Every new piece of technology, every new process in Animation requires change.   There's a real world cost to that.

I first discovered this when i was working at a studio that 280 people.  They were in crisis - the studio had grown too big too fast and they didn't have the infrastructure to handle the growth.  They had the mentality and culture for 40 people.

I brought in ideas that took confusing processes and turnted them into a 90 second process and 1-3 buttons.

We thought everyone would jump for joy!

They didn't.

It caused all kinds of chaos.   Almost impossible to implement.  3 months in we started talking about it as this idea of "change management" being a real part of the process.   After that i have seen it everywhere i've worked, it's a law of moving to a new technology.

At this stage - everything i know about production is written on this website - freely avaiable.  You can even copy and paste some of my articles and build your own pipeline with AI in less than an hour.

The tech isn't hard.   Change management is hard.   Experience is hard.   Knowing the ins and outs of how people react to change and the psychology that drives it is hard.   \

If you’ve worked with me, you’ve heard me say this when I talk about implementing new technology and new processes at a studio.&nbsp;  Typically in the first meeting. \

People typically nod. &nbsp; But they don’t really **understand**.&nbsp; Change management is not just hard, it’s monumentally hard.&nbsp; Here’s some stats - this is somethign that's been studied in the business world forever.

## **Change in Business Literature:**

- **McKinsey & Company:** Decades of McKinsey research consistently show that **70% of organizational change programs fail to reach their stated goals**. They point to employee resistance and a lack of leadership support as the primary causes, rather than tech specs.&nbsp;
- **John Kotter (Harvard Business School):** Kotter’s seminal research behind *Leading Change* notes that **70–85% of major change efforts fail** or default back to old habits because organizations focus on processes instead of **culture and habits.**&nbsp;

## **Change in Production:**

In animation production, bringing in a shiny new production management tool, asset tracker, game engine, or review pipeline often fails because of two major organizational principles:

### **Conway’s Law**

*"Organizations which design systems are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations."* — Melvin Conway (1967)

If a studio or boutique team has chaotic communication, unassigned ownership, or vague review chains, buying a structured software tool will only digitize that chaos. Software enforces existing behavior; it does not invent new behavior.

### **The Law of Conservation of Complexity (Tesler’s Law)**

Larry Tesler (former VP at Apple) argued that every system has an inherent amount of complexity that cannot be removed—it can only be shifted.

When a production company buys software to "simplify" a workflow, they aren't eliminating the complexity of approval chains or missing metadata; they are just shifting the labor onto crew members or artists who now have to populate database fields instead of doing creative work.

## Scale in Production

A studio can only scale to the capacity of its founding leadership.   What does that mean?

Organizations of different sizes require different kinds of expertise, and different underlying systems.

### Heroics Based Studios (~40 people)

- **The Literature:** Larry Greiner’s *Evolution and Revolution as Organizations Grow* (Harvard Business Review) calls this the **Growth Through Creativity** phase.
- **The Dynamics:** Communication is informal, direct, and frequent. Everyone knows what everyone else is doing.

### Tax Haven & Cheap Labor Based Studios (100+)

A studio has 3 levers it can pull when it gets past 100 people.

- Increase Sales
- Lower Costs
- Increase Efficiency

I've written about why studios can't afford to increase efficiency - so the only other options are increasing sales (only possible if you lower costs) and lowering costs.   So the 100+ person company by nature must exist in a tax haven that ALSO has cheap labor.   Why?

To truly operate at above 100 people you need systems.   No 40 person studio runs on systems so when they get to this transition there is no knowledge that they even need systems.  And tools for monitoring business health are essentially a bid and a bank account.

"Are we in the Black" is the only tool i've ever seen at any studio (no matter what the size) to judge business health.   Producers are trained for this.   So at 100+ your only choice is to lower costs with tax havens and cheap labor.   A race to the bottom essentially.

This is why so many studios are going out of business.   We've pulled the lever of lowring costs all over the world in every imaginable way until there's just nowhere else to go.

When the owner of arguably the most successful outsource vendor on the planet tells me he's starting to get the work that used to go to the biggest studios, and he also predicts even he only has 4 more years of runway until his business model no longer works (best tax incentives, cheapest labor) we can't pretend this is a solid business strategy for studios over 100 people anymore.

## The Micro-Studio (Systems Based Studio) (1-100+)

The micro-studio is what i'm calling a modern systems based studio that treats the entire studio as a single organism.  It throws away conway's law and starts from scratch.   The microstudio is the result of all the principles discussed here at moviemanufacturing.com.   It focuses on efficiency.  It focuses on systems.   It knows that making an animated film is a manufacturing process that serves story.   Period.  It measures.   It reacts to what it measures.   Its technology serves its artists.   It's artists are world class - supercharged by tech - not drowning in it.   Its' artists drive the tech.   It starts small.   With people who share the vision.
